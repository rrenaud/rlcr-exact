Scoring rules as pot odds

rlcr-exact · explain-to-expert-poker-player notes · 2026-11-26

Our models answer a question and then state a confidence, a number like 0.7 meaning “I think there is a 70% chance this answer is right.” That is an equity estimate. We train it with a reward, and the reward decides which mistakes in the estimate are expensive. This page makes that concrete for a poker player. Every reasonable reward for a probability is the bankroll of a bettor who faces prices at random, and the reward is pinned down by which prices it faces how often. A poker player facing bets faces a very particular price distribution. Re-scoring our models that way changes what looks broken.

Every proper score is a bettor facing random prices

Take a ticket that pays 1 if the answer is right and costs t. A forecaster who believes c buys it when c > t and passes otherwise. Measured against the ticket’s fair value, the forecaster’s loss on one ticket is:

bought, answer wrong: lose t (paid t for nothing) passed, answer right: lose 1 − t (skipped a ticket worth 1) otherwise: lose 0

Now draw the price t from some distribution w and average. The result is a proper scoring rule: stating your true belief minimizes expected loss, whatever w is. Conversely, every proper scoring rule for yes/no outcomes is one of these averages (Schervish 1989). So choosing a reward is choosing w:

  • Brier score (RLCR’s reward) is twice the average loss when t is uniform on [0, 1]. Every price is equally likely.
  • Log score (rewarding_doubt’s reward, clipped at 0.001) is w(t) ∝ 1/(t(1 − t)). Long shots and near-locks dominate. It is also the growth rate of a Kelly bettor, which is why it punishes “1%” on something that happens 10% of the time so hard.
  • A poker caller. Villain bets b × pot. Calling costs b to win pot + b, so in units of the final pot, the call is a ticket at price t = b / (1 + 2b). That is the pot-odds breakeven equity. If bet sizes run uniformly from a quarter pot to twice the pot, prices fall between 0.167 and 0.40, with more weight toward the top.

A caller never faces a price above one half. Equity estimates above 0.5 never change a calling decision, and neither does any estimate above the largest bet you face. Brier spends most of its weight on prices a caller never sees.

What a bad estimate costs

Suppose your true equity is p and you estimate c. You only make a wrong decision at prices between c and p, and at price t the wrong decision costs |p − t|. The expected cost of the bad estimate is therefore

EV lost(p, c) = ∫ over t between c and p of w(t) · |p − t| dt

That is the whole story of calibration in one line. Two examples a caller will recognise:

  • Overrating a weak hand. True equity 20%, estimate 40%. You call every bet in the range and lose on each one. The caller’s score charges 0.129 final pots per decision. Brier, which mostly faces prices you never see, charges 0.020 (in the same units).
  • Overrating a strong hand. True equity 70%, estimate 95%. You call either way, so the caller is charged nothing. Brier charges 0.031, and the log score 0.023. For a caller this mistake costs no EV.

Free throws: equity you never act on is worth nothing

The Brier page forecast NBA free throws from last season. Under Brier, knowing the shooter helps a little: the in-sample oracle saves 0.0078 Brier per shot over saying the league rate for everyone, or 0.0039 in the price units used here (Brier / 2). Under the caller’s score it saves 0.0003, a twelfth of that. A free throw goes in 78% of the time across the league, and only 126 of 53,432 attempts came from shooters below 0.4. Even a perfect read on the shooter rarely moves a caller off “call”. A poker player knows this instinctively: separating 90% from 70% is useless if both are calls.

Our models, re-scored as callers

The rows are the same HotpotQA dev forecasters as on the Brier page: 500 questions, a greedy answer each, the stated confidence. Each one is scored under all three price distributions, along with the Murphy decomposition generalized to any proper score (S = UNC − RES + REL with each term measured in that score’s EV units) and 5-fold cross-validated isotonic recalibration. Recalibration is the fix a learned map from stated value to hit rate can make without any new information. The isotonic fit is the same for every proper score, so one fit serves all three.

  • Under Brier, our trained runs are calibrated. The rlcr21 conclusion was that recalibration recovers at most about 4% of the score of any exact-arm run, and that the headroom is all discrimination.
  • Scored as callers, several runs are not. Recalibration recovers 11–17% of the caller score for A2 exact s1102 and s2102, B2 exact s1202, B2 rlcr21 s202, and C0 sampled. That is well outside the fold noise of about ±4% seen on runs with nothing to fix.
  • It is one bucket. Almost all of it is the stated value 0.4. In A2 s1102, 72 of 500 answers said 0.4 and 21% of them were right; C0 sampled said 0.4 on 43 answers and was right 16% of the time. A caller with that habit calls every bet up to 2 × pot with 20% equity. The caller’s REL for those runs is 6–9 × its noise floor. Brier barely notices, because a 0.2 gap at one price band is a small slice of its uniform weight.
  • It is per seed more than per arm. Four of the eleven exact-arm runs and one of the seven sampled runs cross 10%. Each arm is at its own pre-registered pick, and these are single dev checkpoints, so this is an observation to test, not a result.
  • Discrimination is still the bigger lever. For the best runs, RES removes 40–50% of the caller’s UNC, and recalibration adds at most another 17% of what is left. But “calibration is solved” turns out to be a statement about Brier’s price distribution, not about every use of the number.

Rewards that model the bet

The price-distribution view gives a recipe for a reward that matches a use. Pick the prices the downstream decision will face, and use the threshold-weighted score with that w. It is proper by construction. It also plugs straight into this project’s exact objective, which needs only a score S(c, y) per confidence string: the expected score under the model’s confidence distribution is a sum over the allowed confidence strings (11 for rlcr11), whatever the score is. Two caveats keep it from being a free win:

  • Zero weight means zero gradient. A pure caller reward is indifferent between stating 0.6 and 0.95, since neither changes a call. The score is proper but not strictly proper, so the model can drift anywhere outside the band. Mixing in a small Brier term keeps every level anchored. The mixing weight would be a new knob to measure against RLCR’s pure Brier default.
  • Calibration is a small lever on a band, not a big lever on the score. The re-scoring above says the caller score has at most about 17% to gain from calibration, even before training on it. That is more than Brier’s 4% but still smaller than the discrimination gap.

Next: a poker dataset with an exact oracle

HotpotQA has a flaw for studying any of this. We never know an answer’s true probability of being right, only whether it was, so every claim above is a statistical estimate from 500 rows. A poker spot does not have that flaw. “Hero holds A♠K♦, the board is Q♠J♦4♣, villain’s range is …: does hero win at showdown?” has an exact answer that an equity calculator can enumerate. The label is a dealt runout, so the outcomes are as noisy as real ones, but the probability behind each label is known. That gives:

  • An exact decomposition. REL and RES against the true equity, not a binned estimate, and a direct readout of whether exact or sampled confidence training learns equity faster.
  • Real discrimination. The earlier random-integer experiment had a calibrated confidence of 1/n by construction, so there was nothing to tell apart. Poker spots span the whole 0–1 range, with a difficulty knob from preflop (coarse) to river (exact).
  • A matching reward. Train with Brier and with the caller score on the same spots and see which one gets the calling band right.

We considered and set aside a synthetic NBA generator: fit a classical model (logistic, Elo) on real games, then sample outcomes from it. Its “truth” is only whatever the fitted model says, so the realism is cosmetic, and an LLM’s priors about real team names would leak into it. Poker gets the same benefits with a true oracle. The first step is cheap and needs no training: ask the base, RLVR and released RLCR models about 1,000 spots and see how far from calibrated they start.

Caveats a poker player will raise

  • Showdown equity is not realized equity. Real calling decisions depend on implied odds, reverse implied odds, and whether you get to see the showdown at all. The caller score here treats each call as a one-shot ticket.
  • Calling is one decision. A bettor or raiser faces a different price distribution, set by villain’s calling range and fold equity. The bet-size range ¼–2 × pot is an assumption chosen for illustration, not measured from hands.
  • The analogy is about the score, not the task. HotpotQA answers are not poker hands. What carries over is only the arithmetic: which prices a decision faces sets which miscalibrations are expensive.

All numbers
